## 4. People, materials, institutions, and choices that drive the account

- Jackson Pollock: maker; developed pouring/dripping onto raw canvas on the ground in 1947. S2.
- Lee Krasner: artist and Pollock’s wife; moved with him to Springs in 1945. The Met says it bought the painting from her in 1957, the year after Pollock’s death. S1-ZH.
- Thomas Hart Benton and David Alfaro Siqueiros: the Met identifies Pollock’s large-scale work as rooted in 1930s muralism and notes that he had worked alongside both. This supports historical continuity, not a claim that either determined this painting’s individual marks. S1.
- Barn studio at Springs: enabled floor-based work; Pollock-Krasner House records that Pollock laid canvases on the floor, walked around them, and applied liquid paint from all four sides. S3.
- Hans Namuth: photographed Pollock at the edge of this canvas; another MoMA image specifically identifies Pollock adding white paint to the lower part in late summer 1950. S9, S10.
- The Metropolitan Museum of Art: acquired the work in 1957. The Met interprets the timing—one year after Pollock’s death—as evidence of how quickly his reinvention entered the modern-art canon. S1.

## 5. Evidence-backed causal chain

1. **Mural-scale problem:** Pollock stated in 1947 that he intended to make large movable pictures functioning between easel painting and mural; the Met connects his large scale to his earlier contact with Benton and Siqueiros. S1.
2. **Working environment:** at Springs, Pollock used a barn studio and preferred canvases on the floor, allowing access from four sides rather than a single frontal easel position. S3.
3. **Technical change:** in 1947 he developed pouring and dripping thinned paint onto raw canvas laid on the ground. S2.
4. **Material consequence:** liquid paint applied from above could form lines, splashes, pools, and variable widths without normal brush-to-canvas contact; the present image preserves those distinct mark types. S2, S4, O2–O6.
5. **1950 concentration:** MoMA identifies *Autumn Rhythm*, *One: Number 31*, and *Number 32* as three monumental canvases made in summer/fall 1950; *One* was made when Pollock was at the height of his career. S5, S6.
6. **Work-specific documentation:** Namuth photographed Pollock at the edge of *Autumn Rhythm* and adding white paint to its lower part. This allows a limited process-to-surface link for that white passage; it does not authorize reconstruction of the full layer sequence. S9, S10.
7. **Historical consequence:** acquisition by the Met in 1957, one year after Pollock’s death, is presented by the museum as a sign of rapid canonical acceptance. S1.

## 6. Artistic mechanism and art-historical value

### Mechanism

- Canvas orientation/process: raw, unstretched canvas placed on the ground; access from multiple sides. S2, S3.
- Application: pouring, dripping, spattering, flinging, and pooling with unconventional tools such as sticks, brush handles, and cans. S4.
- Physical variables supported by museum sources: gravity, momentum, force, speed, paint viscosity/flow, and movement around the support. S6, S8.
- Organization visible in this work:
  - broad distribution of incidents rather than a single central motif;
  - variable dark and light lines cross one another;
  - broad brown deposits and narrow linear trails coexist;
  - unpainted/buff ground remains visible between marks;
  - local density varies even though activity extends across the field. O1–O7.
- Evidence boundary: visible overlap alone cannot establish the complete chronological order of all colors. The only work-specific sequence detail retained here is Namuth’s caption identifying white added to the lower part in late summer 1950. S10.

### Art-historical value

- The Met describes Pollock’s 1947 ground-based pouring/dripping as a radical technique and places it within mature gestural Abstract Expressionism. S2.
- The work tests the boundary Pollock articulated between easel picture and mural-sized wall picture. S1.
- The Met’s related drawing record describes Pollock’s poured works as redefining traditional painting and drawing because line became the primary expressive vehicle despite the use of paint. S4.
- MoMA’s retrospective groups this work with two other monumental 1950 canvases and treats the trio as the culmination of the classic 1947–50 poured paintings. S5.
- Broader influence should be stated at the level of Pollock’s approach, not attributed solely to this canvas: MoMA documents influence on Gutai, Happenings, and later large-scale, holistic practices. S11.
